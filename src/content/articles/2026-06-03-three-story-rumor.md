A two-storey commercial block on a downtown stretch of Uxbridge is reportedly being demolished to make way for a three-storey replacement, according to a Facebook post that has, in the space of eighteen minutes, transformed an entirely unremarkable building into a cherished community landmark.

"I heard this building is coming down," wrote one local woman in a regional advocacy group, attaching a Google Street View image of the structure in question. "Does any one have any deets on this?"

The post, which has since been viewed a number of times consistent with how many people are on Facebook in this town at 7:14 p.m. on a weeknight, has generated a wide range of responses including:

- Three (3) people speculating, with no apparent basis, about which property-management entity is responsible.
- One (1) person posting a single period as a comment, a regional convention understood to mean both *"I am following this thread"* and *"I have nothing to add but want you to know I am here."*
- One (1) person observing that the building "isn't that aesthetically pleasing," a sentiment that, prior to the rumour of its demolition, had been the consensus position for approximately forty years.

At press time, no resident has produced any documentary evidence of an approved development application. The original post itself acknowledges this, asking only whether the structure has to "conform to any aesthetic," a phrasing that local linguists describe as **load-bearing.**

## The aesthetic in question

The phrase "any aesthetic" refers to an unspoken municipal compact wherein new construction in the downtown core is expected to gesture, however faintly, at the brick storefronts that comprise the rest of the streetscape. Residents are particularly concerned that the replacement will instead resemble a **recently completed infill structure on a nearby block**, widely agreed to be an aesthetic catastrophe of the kind that produces a small, involuntary sigh every time a longtime resident drives past it.

"That building," said one downtown shopkeeper, declining to specify which downtown shopkeeper, "is what we got the *last* time someone said the words 'conforms to the surrounding character.'"

She declined to elaborate further on the grounds that she still has to walk past it.

## A complicated grief

Reached for comment Wednesday morning, several residents acknowledged that the building under threat is, by any reasonable measure, also not very nice to look at. It contains a variety store, several upper-floor offices, and the kind of beige render that has, since installation, slowly assumed the colour of a damp afternoon.

"Look, I'm not saying it was nice," said a woman who has lived in the area since before the new subdivision. "I'm saying it was *there*. It was familiar. It was a building. We knew where it was. Now they want to put up something taller, and I can already feel myself emailing the Township about it."

A neighbouring resident, asked whether she would prefer the existing building to remain, paused for what witnesses described as "a really long time" before saying: "I would prefer that the *concept* of the existing building remain. The actual building can do whatever it wants."

This sentiment — that what is being mourned is not the structure itself but the *idea* of having known the structure, and the deeper *idea* of nothing in particular ever changing downtown — has been identified by regional sociologists as the dominant emotional register of the small-Ontario-town Facebook group, second only to mild outrage about geese.

## Officials offer clarity, are ignored

A Township spokesperson, reached for what was characterized in the comments as "the actual deets," confirmed that any redevelopment proposal would be subject to standard zoning review, public consultation, and the usual conditions regarding setbacks, façade treatment, and signage.

The spokesperson noted that, to date, the Township has received **no formal application** matching the description being circulated.

This clarification has been received warmly and incorporated into the discussion as follows: it has not.

"They always say that," wrote one commenter, in a sentence that, taken in aggregate across the region's civic Facebook groups, has now been written approximately one hundred and forty thousand times. "Then six months later there's a hole in the ground."

By Wednesday evening, the thread had progressed to its mature phase, in which the original question — *does anyone have any deets* — has been forgotten, and the discussion has bifurcated into:

1. People asking what was on the site before the current building (consensus: something).
2. People declaring that they have lived here for many years and have therefore earned the right to be slightly annoyed about this.

The building, for its part, remains standing, beige, and entirely unaware that it has, in the past hour, been promoted from "that one with the variety store" to "a piece of our downtown character that we are at risk of losing forever."

A passing terrier was unavailable for comment.
